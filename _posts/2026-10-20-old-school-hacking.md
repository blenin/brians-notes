Before the hoodie. Before Hollywood's green code raining down a screen. Before "hacker" became a word that makes banks nervous and grandparents lock their laptops — there were hackers. And they would barely recognize what the word means today.

The original hackers weren't criminals. They were students at MIT in the late 1950s and 60s who couldn't leave a system alone until they understood it completely — and then pushed it to do things its designers never imagined. One quick note before we start: there's no movement actually called "Ola School" hacking. What exists, and what this article is about, is **old school hacking** — the culture, principles, and way of thinking that quietly built much of the world we now live in.

## First, a definition the media ruined

The *Jargon File*, the hacker community's own dictionary, defines a hacker as "a person who enjoys exploring the details of programmable systems and stretching their capabilities, as opposed to most users, who prefer to learn only the minimum necessary."

Eric S. Raymond put it even more sharply: **hackers build things, crackers break them.** Being able to break security, he argued, makes you a hacker no more than being able to hotwire a car makes you an automotive engineer.

That distinction matters, because old school hacking started as creation, not destruction. At MIT's Tech Model Railroad Club — a club for model trains, of all things — members obsessed over the elaborate switching systems under the layout. When the university got early computers like the TX-0 and the PDP-1, the same people applied the same obsession to them. The result? *Spacewar!*, one of the first video games that mattered, written not for profit but because the machine was there and it could be done.

## The phone system was the first playground

In the late 1960s, the most complex system on Earth wasn't a computer. It was the telephone network.

AT&T's long-distance system used tones to control itself — including a 2600 Hz tone that could seize a long-distance line. A loosely connected underground of "phone phreaks" figured this out by ear, by experiment, and by sharing notes. John Draper, known as Captain Crunch (a toy whistle from a cereal box, associated with Draper and his friends, produced exactly that 2600 Hz tone), built "blue boxes" that reproduced the phone company's control tones electronically.

When *Esquire* magazine profiled the phreaks in 1971, Draper explained the motive in one sentence that still defines the mindset:

> "I'm learning about a system. The phone company is a System. A computer is a System… If I do what I do, it is only to explore a system."

That *Esquire* article, by the way, is what pushed two young Californians named Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs to build and sell blue boxes before they founded Apple. The line from phreaking to personal computing is not a metaphor. It's direct.

The culture spread through the Homebrew Computer Club (founded 1975), where hobbyists handed out their schematics for free — Wozniak literally set the Apple I designs on a table for anyone to take. Then came dial-up bulletin board systems (the first, CBBS, launched in Chicago in 1978), the magazine *2600: The Hacker Quarterly* (1984, named after that tone), and the electronic magazine *Phrack* (1985). Knowledge moved from hand to hand, modem to modem.

## The principles: Levy's Hacker Ethic

In 1984, journalist Steven Levy published *Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution*. Like an anthropologist studying a tribe, he distilled the culture's unspoken assumptions into six tenets — the famous **Hacker Ethic**:

1. **Access to computers — and anything which might teach you something about the way the world works — should be unlimited and total. Always yield to the Hands-On Imperative!**
2. **All information should be free.**
3. **Mistrust authority — promote decentralization.**
4. **Hackers should be judged by their hacking, not criteria such as degrees, age, race, sex, or position.**
5. **You can create art and beauty on a computer.**
6. **Computers can change your life for the better.**

Read those again and notice what they are *not*. They are not "steal data." They are not "cause damage." They are a philosophy of learning: touch the machine yourself, share what you find, distrust gatekeepers, judge people by their work, and use computers to make life better.

Two years later, a hacker called The Mentor — arrested, and writing after the movie *WarGames* had painted hackers as reckless kids who might start a nuclear war — published "The Conscience of a Hacker" in *Phrack*: "We explore… and you call us criminals. We seek after knowledge… and you call us criminals."

## The ethics: a real code, honestly told

Old school culture did have a code, even if nobody ever signed it. Summarized by *2600*'s own tradition: hackers **should not damage or profit from the systems they crack** — the idea being that exposing a vulnerability was a public service, not a business or a weapon. Members of Masters of Deception, the famous New York group, had their own version: **"Thou shalt not destroy. It's OK to look around, but don't hurt anything."**

Now the honest part — because this story is often told too cleanly:

- **The ethic was never unanimous.** Masters of Deception's own Acid Phreak said it plainly: "There is no one hacker ethic."
- **Exploration wasn't legal just because the motive was curiosity.** Phreaking was, in practice, toll fraud — Draper went to jail for it. Some hackers copied proprietary software and read private files. The stated ethic condemned destruction and profit; real life was messier.
- **"Leave no trace," which you'll see in a hundred modern articles, is not in Levy's six tenets** or in the classic sources. It's a later summary of the do-no-harm idea — not a rule the old hackers actually wrote down.

The law, meanwhile, caught up. The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (a first law in 1984, strengthened in 1986) criminalized unauthorized access in the US. In 1988, Robert Morris released a self-replicating worm meant, he said, to measure the internet. A flaw in its logic crippled roughly 6,000 of the internet's 60,000 machines in a day. Morris became the first person criminally convicted under the CFAA — and the incident led directly to the creation of the first computer emergency response team, CERT.

By the early 1990s the scene split in two. Some members of the Legion of Doom formed a security company, Comsec, in 1990 — selling defense instead of breaking in. Five members of Masters of Deception pleaded guilty to federal charges. Same skills, same curiosity. Two opposite futures.

## The mindset: how an old school hacker thinks

Strip away the era and the equipment, and what's left is a way of thinking that is still valuable today:

- **Hands first.** You don't really know a system by reading about it. You know it by touching it, breaking it, and fixing it. Levy called it the Hands-On Imperative.
- **A system is a system.** Phone network, computer, bureaucracy — the hacker sees formal, rule-based systems everywhere, and asks the same question: *I know what the rules say. What else do they allow?* One account of Levy's hackers describes the core joy as "manipulating the functioning of formal, rule-based systems to produce unanticipated results." That sentence is the whole game: understanding rules so completely that you can see the possibilities hiding inside them.
- **Intimate understanding before cleverness.** Tricks fail. Deep knowledge doesn't. The old hackers spent weeks shaving single instructions off a routine just to prove it could be done more elegantly — patience as a competitive sport.
- **Sharing is a duty.** Knowledge hoarded was knowledge wasted. Schematics on a table at Homebrew, code left open at MIT, techniques published in *Phrack* — reputation came from what you gave away, not what you kept.
- **Titles mean nothing.** No degrees, no permission slips. At MIT, what mattered was the hack itself. That meritocracy — imperfect, but real — is why teenagers on BBSs could earn the respect of engineers.

## Divide and conquer: the hacker's oldest strategy

Here's where everything above comes together. And a small honesty note first: "divide and conquer" is **not** a phrase you'll find as a formal doctrine in the old hacker texts, and modern frameworks like MITRE ATT&CK don't use it as a category. But as a way of describing *how hacking actually works* — then and now — nothing fits better. It works in four directions.

### 1. Divide the problem

Nobody "hacks a network." A network is too big, too unknown, too defended to attack as one blob. So the hacker divides it: What machines exist? What does each one run? Which services are exposed? Which single piece looks weakest or most interesting?

One host at a time. One service at a time. One question at a time. The Milwaukee teenagers known as the 414s, caught by the FBI in 1983, didn't defeat some monolithic system — they worked system by system, reading each target's own manuals and trying what they learned. Modern security testing still follows exactly this shape: reconnaissance, then enumeration, then testing one weakness at a time.

Incident investigators use the identical method from the defensive side. Digital forensics teams literally call it a "divide and conquer process": take the huge question — *is this machine compromised?* — and split it into smaller questions until each one can be answered by examining a single category of evidence.

### 2. Divide the defenses

Once inside a network, an intruder's first machine is rarely the prize. So the conquest proceeds in pieces, alternating two moves that security professionals do have formal names for:

- **Lateral movement** — the horizontal move. Pivot from the machine you control to the next one, often using legitimate credentials and built-in tools, which makes the movement hard to distinguish from normal work.
- **Privilege escalation** — the vertical move. On a machine you already control, climb from ordinary user to administrator.

Move sideways until you find a machine where you can climb. Climb, then use the new privileges to move further sideways. Piece by piece, a whole network falls — not because its wall was smashed, but because it was divided into rooms and each room was taken separately.

History's clearest example is trust itself. The Morris Worm spread partly because machines were configured to trust each other — logins from a trusted machine needed no password. Conquer one machine, and the trust of its neighbors came with it. Each system trusted the last, so the defense of the whole was only as strong as its weakest piece.

And the division isn't only technical. Kevin Mitnick, the most famous hacker of the 1990s, testified to the US Senate that he rarely needed a technical attack at all. He worked people **one at a time**: call one employee, learn the internal lingo and system names, then use that familiarity to convince the next employee he belonged. He called people "the weakest link in the security chain." One person is a small problem. A company of ten thousand people, approached one by one, is ten thousand small problems.

### 3. Divide the search

Divide and conquer is also a formal idea in computer science: split a problem into smaller, similar subproblems, solve each, and combine the results. It's how merge sort orders a list and how binary search finds a value by ruling out half the possibilities at every step.

Security work runs on the same shape. Scanning a network means dividing it by host, then by port, then by service. And password cracking at scale is, in effect, a divided search: distributed cracking systems split the enormous space of possible passwords into slices, assign each slice to a different machine, and carefully track which candidates have been tried so no work is wasted. The defense is the mirror image — modern password hashing is deliberately *slow* and salted, so that every single slice of that divided search becomes expensive.

### 4. The defenders learned to divide first

Here is the twist that completes the story: modern defense is built on dividing *before* the attacker can.

It's called **segmentation**. Split the network into small zones — by department, by function, by sensitivity — and enforce strict, least-privilege rules between them. Isolate the crown jewels, like domain controllers and critical databases. The zero trust model (formalized in NIST SP 800-207) states it as a motto: *never trust, always verify.* No machine is trusted just because it's "inside."

Why does it work? Because it removes the attacker's path. If a compromised laptop in accounting physically cannot open a connection to the engineering servers, there is no next hop — no matter what credentials the attacker holds. Credentials that unlock one segment do not unlock its neighbor.

So both sides divide. The attacker divides the target into conquerable pieces. The defender divides the network into defensible pieces, and the investigation into answerable questions. Whoever divides more carefully usually wins.

## Myth vs. reality

Hollywood gets almost all of it wrong. Real hacking is not thirty seconds of frantic typing and a triumphant "I'm in." It's hours of reconnaissance, reading, testing, and waiting — more librarian than action hero. The lone-wolf image is wrong too: old school hacking was intensely *social*, built on clubs, bulletin boards, magazines, and groups with shared handles and reputations. And the idea that the original hackers were vandals is backwards — Levy's hackers, in the words of a later *Communications of the ACM* retrospective, were "neither destructive nor dedicated to the pilfering of proprietary data."

Even the era's biggest myth had a real effect: after watching *WarGames* in 1983 — a teenager nearly starting World War III with a modem — President Reagan reportedly asked whether it could really happen. The answer helped trigger the first US presidential directive on computer security. A movie invented the scenario; the policy was real.

## What the old school left us

Almost everything around you, it turns out:

- **Personal computing** — the Homebrew-to-Apple pipeline is only the most famous example.
- **Free and open-source software** — Levy's "all information should be free" grew up into Linux, the free software movement, and the open-source world that now runs most of the internet.
- **The security profession itself** — the L0pht, a Boston hacker collective, testified before the US Senate in 1998 that they could shut down the internet in 30 minutes, then became a legitimate security company. Today's penetration testers, bug bounties (the modern template dates to Netscape in 1995), and responsible disclosure programs are the old ethic with one crucial upgrade: **permission**.
- **The culture** — DEF CON (founded 1993), capture-the-flag competitions, hackathons (the word was coined in 1999), and makerspaces all descend directly from clubs and BBSs where people gathered to take things apart together.
- **The watchdog** — the Electronic Frontier Foundation was founded in 1990, in direct response to the crackdown era. The raids meant to end hacker culture ended up giving it a legal defense fund.

## The honest lesson

The old school hackers were not saints, and this article hasn't tried to make them into any. Some broke laws, some went to prison, and curiosity was never the legal defense they hoped it would be. The Mentor himself was writing *after an arrest*.

But separate the crimes from the mindset, and the mindset is gold: Learn systems by touching them. Understand the rules so well you can see what else they allow. Judge work, not titles. Share what you learn. And when a problem looks impossibly large — a network, a company, a machine you've never seen — don't attack it whole.

Divide it. Understand one piece completely. Then the next.

That's not just how the old hackers explored the world. It's how they ended up *changing* it — for the better, which was tenet number six all along.
